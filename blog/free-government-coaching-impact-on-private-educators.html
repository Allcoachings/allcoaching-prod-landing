Will Free Government Coaching Hurt My Coaching Institute?

On 15 August 2026, from the Red Fort, the Prime Minister announced free online coaching for competitive exams, saying coaching classes burden the poor and the middle class, and that the government would combine digital public infrastructure with India's teachers to deliver it. No scheme name, mechanism or timeline was announced. Here is what that actually changes for a private educator — and, more usefully, what it does not.

By Amit Ratan
Founder & CEO, AllCoaching
Published August 15, 2026  ·  20 min read  ·  Strategy

Free content has been competing with Indian coaching for more than a decade. It never won, because what a family pays for was never the content — it was someone being answerable for their child's result.

The reframe

This was an announcement
about cost, not competition.

Free government coaching, announced on 15 August 2026, is unlikely to displace private coaching in India — but it will accelerate the commoditisation of one specific business model within it: the one that sells recorded syllabus coverage to large impersonal batches and justifies its price by access to material rather than by responsibility for a result. If that is not your model, the announcement changes your competitive position far less than the last two days of commentary suggests.

Read the Prime Minister's actual words carefully, because the framing matters. The statement was "coaching classes burden the poor and the middle class" — a sentence about household financial strain, not about the quality or legitimacy of private teaching. The problem being addressed is that families who cannot afford coaching feel they must buy it anyway. That is a cost-of-access problem, and the intervention aims at students who are currently priced out — students who, by definition, were not paying you.

Almost every anxious conversation happening among coaching owners this week collapses those two things: an announcement about affordability is being processed as an announcement about competition. They are different. A public provision aimed at people who cannot pay does not automatically take customers from people who can and do. It changes the floor of the market, not necessarily its middle. Whether it touches you depends entirely on what, precisely, your students are paying for — which is the question the rest of this analysis is built around.

· · ·

The record

What was actually said —
and what was not.

Precision here is worth more than speed, because a great deal of confident commentary is currently being extrapolated from a small number of sentences. In his Independence Day address from the Red Fort — the 80th Independence Day, his thirteenth consecutive address, delivered under the theme Yuva Shakti for Viksit Bharat@2047 — the Prime Minister announced free online coaching for competitive examinations[1].

"Coaching classes burden the poor and the middle class. We will provide free online coaching for various exams."

— Prime Minister Narendra Modi, Independence Day address, 15 August 2026, as reported

Reported alongside it were three further points worth holding onto. First, the reasoning: intense competition around coaching has become a serious financial burden on middle-class families, with parents feeling that not sending a child to coaching means the child falls behind[2]. Second, the mechanism: the government would draw on India's digital public infrastructure together with its pool of teachers and educators to deliver the initiative[3]. Third, the surrounding context: the same address committed to training one crore young people in artificial intelligence over the following year.

What has not been announced, at the time of writing: a scheme name, the delivery mechanism in any operational detail, a timeline, eligibility criteria, which examinations are covered, how teachers would be selected or compensated, or how it interacts with existing platforms. Any article telling you confidently what this will do to your admissions is guessing.

This absence is not a criticism — Independence Day addresses state direction, and ministries publish specifics later. But it should govern how you respond. Plan for the direction of travel; do not restructure your institute around details that do not yet exist. The direction of travel is clear enough on its own: the state considers the cost of exam preparation a public problem, and intends to use shared digital rails and existing teachers rather than build a single central institute. Both halves of that matter, and the second half is the one educators are under-reading.

The precedent

Free has been your competitor
for a decade.

The single most useful corrective to this week's anxiety is historical. Free, high-quality educational content has existed at enormous scale in India for more than a decade, and paid coaching grew anyway. This is not a prediction; it is the record. Any theory that free supply destroys paid coaching has to explain why it has not already happened.

Consider what is already free and has been for years. NCERT textbooks — the actual foundation of most competitive syllabi — are freely available. SWAYAM hosts free courses spanning school to higher education, built by the Government of India. DIKSHA serves school content nationally. And beyond government provision, YouTube contains more free JEE, NEET, UPSC and SSC instruction than any student could consume in a lifetime, much of it taught by genuinely excellent teachers. A motivated student with a smartphone has had free access to world-class instruction for years.

And yet Indian families kept paying. Not because they were unaware of the free options — they were entirely aware — but because free content solved a problem they did not have. They did not lack access to explanations. They lacked something else, and identifying that something is the whole of a coaching educator's defensibility.

Question Often Asked

If free YouTube coaching never hurt us, why would government free coaching be different?

In three ways it could be, and it is worth being honest about them rather than dismissing the question. First, trust and legitimacy — a government-backed offering carries an institutional credibility that an unknown YouTube channel does not, particularly for parents in smaller towns who are cautious about online learning. Second, structure — if the initiative delivers organised, sequenced, exam-specific programmes rather than scattered videos, it addresses the main weakness of free content, which has always been that students do not know what to watch in what order. Third, scale of awareness — a national announcement reaches families who never searched for free alternatives at all. Those are real differences and a serious educator should account for them. What none of them changes is the core limitation: a national free programme, however well built, cannot be personally answerable for one student's result. It can teach a million students; it cannot notice that one of them stopped submitting tests in October — and noticing exactly that is the discipline examined in reducing student dropout in online coaching.

The real product

What an Indian family
actually pays you for.

Ask a parent in Kanpur or Patna why they pay coaching fees and the answer is almost never "for the lectures". They are buying accountability — the reasonable expectation that a named adult is paying attention to their specific child and will notice when something goes wrong. The syllabus explanation is the visible part of the transaction. It is not the valuable part, and it has not been the valuable part for years.

Break down what actually transfers when a student joins a good coaching class. There is structure — a sequence, a calendar, a decision about what to study when, which removes the paralysing question of where to start. There is correction — someone who sees the mistake in the student's method, not just the wrong answer, and intervenes. There is discipline — the simple, underrated fact that a fixed class at a fixed hour with a teacher who notices absence produces attendance that a free video library never will. And there is social accountability — peers, ranks, a teacher's expectation.

What free content supplies well

Explanation of concepts · syllabus coverage · worked examples · revision material · exam-pattern familiarity · unlimited repetition at zero marginal cost.

What free content structurally cannot supply

Personal accountability for one student's result · noticing when a specific student disengages · correcting an individual's method rather than their answer · a relationship a family trusts · the discipline of being expected somewhere at a fixed time.

This distinction is the entire strategic question, and it is worth stating as a test: if a student could replace your course with a free playlist and lose nothing, you were selling information. If they would lose the thing that actually makes them study — attention, correction, expectation — you were selling accountability, and free supply does not reach it. Most genuinely good educators are in the second category and have simply never articulated it, which is why they feel threatened by every announcement of free content. The economics of pricing that value are worked through in how to price online courses in India.

The honest part

Who is genuinely
at risk.

A comforting article would stop at "nothing changes". That would be dishonest, and educators deserve better. There is a segment of Indian coaching for which this announcement is a real, structural threat, and naming it plainly is more useful than reassurance.

The exposed model has a recognisable shape. It sells recorded syllabus coverage as its primary product. It runs large batches where no individual student is known by the teacher. Its pricing is justified by access — you pay to get the lectures — rather than by outcomes or attention. And it competes chiefly on being cheaper than the larger branded player rather than on being better than anyone. For that operator, a credible free national alternative attacks the only axis on which they were competing, and price is not an axis you can defend against free.

Exposed

Content-as-product. Recorded lectures, large anonymous batches, price justified by access, competing on being cheaper.

Defensible

Accountability-as-product. Known students, correction and attention, price justified by outcomes, competing on being trusted.

There is a second, subtler exposure worth flagging: the institute whose enrolment depends on being the only visible option in its locality. Geographic monopoly has quietly protected a great many coaching centres — not because they were the best available teaching, but because they were the available teaching. Every expansion of credible online supply, free or paid, erodes that protection a little further. This has been happening for years and this announcement continues it rather than starting it. The response is not to resist it but to stop depending on it, a shift discussed in migrating offline coaching online at zero cost.

The other side

Who this may
quietly help.

Large expansions of free supply have a consistent second-order effect that is easy to miss: they grow the population comfortable with the format. When a national initiative tells millions of families that serious exam preparation happens online, it normalises online learning for households that would never otherwise have tried it — and a meaningful share of those families, having started free, will pay for more attention once they are convinced the format works.

This is the pattern seen wherever free tiers meet paid markets, and India's own recent history supports it. The years of free YouTube instruction did not shrink the paid coaching market; they expanded the number of people who believed online learning was legitimate, and the paid market grew alongside. Free supply tends to convert non-consumers into consumers, and only a portion of those stop at free. The educator positioned to receive that overflow — visible, credible, offering the attention that free provision cannot — is a beneficiary rather than a casualty.

There is also a compositional effect. When basic content is free and abundant, the question a student asks shifts from "where can I get the material" to "who should I learn from". That is a question about the teacher, not the institution — and it is a far better question for an individually excellent educator than for a brand whose advantage was advertising budget. Abundance of content moves the competition onto ground where a genuinely good teacher wins. Building the recognition that lets a student answer that question in your favour is the work described in building a personal brand as an educator in India, and the practical routes to first paid students without ad spend are in how to get paid students for online coaching free.

Free content does not compete with a great teacher. It competes with a mediocre institution that was charging for access to information — and it removes the cover that institution was hiding behind.

The clause to reread

The DPI clause, and why
educators should read it twice.

The most consequential phrase in the announcement is not "free" — it is the delivery mechanism. The government said it would draw on India's digital public infrastructure together with its pool of teachers and educators[3]. That is a structural statement, and it deserves more attention than the headline.

Digital public infrastructure means shared, open rails built as public utilities — the architectural approach India has used at national scale for identity and payments. The defining property of that approach is that it does not replace private providers; it connects them. Payments infrastructure did not eliminate banks and merchants — it made it possible for millions of small participants to transact on common rails, and the biggest beneficiaries were the smallest players, who gained reach they could never have built alone.

Applied to education, the same logic points somewhere specific. A DPI-shaped approach to coaching implies connecting India's existing teachers through shared infrastructure rather than employing a few of them in a central institution. It is a networked answer, not a monopoly answer. And a networked answer requires exactly one thing from an individual educator: to be discoverable and structured enough to be connected to. An educator who exists only as an offline classroom, or as an isolated personal app nobody can find, is not connectable by any network, public or private.

Question Often Asked

Should I wait to see the scheme's details before changing anything about my coaching?

Wait before restructuring; do not wait before strengthening. The distinction matters. Any decision that depends on the scheme's specifics — pricing overhauls, changing your exam focus, closing a centre — should absolutely wait, because no scheme name, mechanism, timeline or exam list has been published, and acting on speculation is how institutes make expensive mistakes. But the actions worth taking are ones you would want regardless of what the scheme turns out to be: knowing your students individually, being able to prove your results, making sure a student searching for your subject in your city can find you, and being discoverable on more than one channel. None of those becomes wrong under any version of the scheme, and all of them take months to build — which is why starting them now and deciding later is the correct sequence rather than a contradiction.

The response

The 7-step response for
the next 12 months.

None of these depends on the scheme's details. All of them are worth doing whatever it turns out to be — which is precisely why they are the right things to start now.

1

Step 01

Audit what is information and what is accountability

List everything a student pays you for. Mark each as information (replicable by any free platform) or accountability (not replicable). The ratio tells you your actual exposure.

2

Step 02

Name the outcome you own

Free platforms serve everyone and are answerable for no one. State which exam, which cohort and which result your teaching is accountable for — that sentence is your defensibility.

3

Step 03

Shift from batch size to attention density

Competing on cheapest-large-batch is competing on the axis free supply attacks. Smaller cohorts with real attention are harder to replicate and hold their price.

4

Step 04

Build visible, honest proof

Improvement records, selections, consented testimonials. When content is abundant, proof is the scarce currency — and it is the thing no free platform can borrow from you.

5

Step 05

Fix discovery before capacity

Most capable educators do not have a teaching problem; they have a being-found problem. Make sure students searching your subject and city can reach you without you buying ads.

6

Step 06

Reprice on outcome, not on access

If your fee is justified by access to lectures, free supply erodes it. If it is justified by attention and accountability, it holds. Say so explicitly to parents.

7

Step 07

Review when details are published

Follow the Ministry of Education for specifics rather than headlines, then revisit this assessment. Adjust to facts, not to speculation.

The binding constraint

The constraint that
decides your outcome.

Underneath every version of this question sits one constraint that has decided Indian educators' fortunes for a decade, and it is not content and not price. It is discovery — whether a student who does not already know you can find you. The announcement changes the content landscape. It does not change the fact that a superb teacher in Prayagraj is invisible to a student in Ranchi who would have paid them gladly.

This is where a great deal of well-intentioned advice fails educators. Being told to "improve quality" is useless to someone whose quality was never the problem — and in our experience across the AllCoaching educator base in 2026, it rarely is. The teachers we onboard are typically excellent and typically unknown. They spent ₹30,000–40,000 on a personal app that became, in the manifesto's phrase, an island: built, branded, and completely undiscoverable. The app was never the missing piece. The myths

Three myths circulating
since the speech.

Three claims have spread quickly this week, and each misreads what was said.

Myth

"The government is going to shut down private coaching."

Reality

Nothing announced restricts private coaching. The statement was about providing a free option for families burdened by cost, not about prohibiting paid teaching.

The second myth is "we should cut fees immediately" — a reflex that damages economics permanently in response to a scheme whose details do not yet exist. Discounting is the right answer only if your offering genuinely competes on access to content, in which case the offering is the problem and the price is a symptom. The third is "this only affects the big edtech brands" — comforting and wrong in the opposite direction, because a large brand has capital and distribution to absorb a shock, while a small centre competing purely on being the cheapest local source of lectures has neither. Exposure follows business model, not size.

The verdict

The verdict.

So the honest answer to whether free government coaching will hurt your coaching institute is: it depends on what you were actually selling, and most educators have never been forced to answer that precisely. If you were selling access to information, the answer is yes, and it was already becoming yes before 15 August — NCERT, SWAYAM, DIKSHA and a decade of free YouTube instruction had been eroding that position for years. If you were selling accountability for a specific student's result, the answer is no, because no national programme, however well funded, can be answerable for one child.

What the announcement genuinely does is remove an excuse. For years, a coaching centre could survive on being the only visible option locally, or on being slightly cheaper than a big brand, without ever articulating what it was actually worth. An abundant free alternative makes that articulation compulsory. That is uncomfortable, and it is also the most useful thing that could happen to an educator who is genuinely good and has simply never had to say why.

And read the mechanism once more before deciding how to feel. The stated approach is to connect India's teachers through shared digital rails — a networked answer that requires individual educators to be discoverable, not a central institution that replaces them. The educators who do well over the next decade will be the ones who are findable on their merit rather than their marketing budget. That was true before this speech; the speech simply made it national policy language.

  • Do audit information versus accountability — the ratio is your real exposure.
  • Do not cut fees reflexively — discounting is a symptom fix for a positioning problem.
  • Do build proof of outcomes — the scarce currency when content is abundant.

Glossary

Glossary —
key terms.

Term

Digital Public Infrastructure (DPI)

Shared, open digital systems built and governed as public utilities, such as national identity, payment and data-exchange layers. In education its significance is that it enables many independent providers to be connected through common rails rather than replaced by a single central provider.

Term

Information versus Accountability

The distinction between supplying content and being answerable for an individual student's result. Free platforms scale information to everyone; a coaching educator's defensible value is accountability for a specific student, which does not scale and therefore does not commoditise.

Term

Attention Density

The amount of individual teacher attention a student actually receives, measured per student rather than per batch. It is the variable free content cannot replicate, and the one that most reliably justifies a fee once information itself is abundant.

Term

Commoditisation

The process by which a product becomes indistinguishable across providers and competes only on price. Recorded syllabus coverage in Indian coaching has been commoditising for a decade; personal instruction and accountability have not.

Term

Discovery

The mechanism by which a student who does not already know an educator comes to find them. It is supplied by search, referral or a marketplace, and it is the binding constraint for most capable independent educators — distinct from teaching quality, which they usually already have.

Term

SWAYAM

A Government of India online platform hosting free courses from school through higher education. It is one of several long-standing free public education offerings, alongside DIKSHA and NCERT material, whose existence explains why free supply alone has not displaced paid coaching.

Term

Educator Marketplace

A shared platform where many independent educators are individually discoverable by students, as distinct from a single institution employing teachers or an isolated personal app with no inbound student traffic. Its defining property is that student demand compounds across educators.

Term

Outcome-Based Positioning

Justifying a fee by the specific result and accountability an educator provides rather than by access to learning material. It is the positioning least vulnerable to free content, because the thing being sold was never the content.

FAQ

Frequently asked
questions.

What exactly did the Prime Minister announce on 15 August 2026?

In his Independence Day address from the Red Fort, Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced free online coaching for competitive examinations, saying that coaching classes burden the poor and the middle class. He said the government would draw on India's digital public infrastructure together with its pool of teachers and educators to deliver it. The address also included a commitment to train one crore young people in artificial intelligence over the following year, under the theme Yuva Shakti for Viksit Bharat at 2047.

Has the government published details of the free coaching scheme?

Not at the time of writing. No scheme name, delivery mechanism, timeline, eligibility criteria or list of covered examinations has been announced alongside the statement. This matters, because almost every confident prediction currently circulating about the scheme's impact is extrapolating from a single sentence. The responsible position for an educator is to plan for the direction of travel rather than react to details that do not yet exist.

Will free government coaching end private coaching in India?

It is very unlikely to end private coaching, because free educational supply has already existed at enormous scale for over a decade without displacing it. YouTube, SWAYAM, DIKSHA and NCERT material are all free and widely available, yet Indian families continue to pay coaching educators. What families pay for is not access to information but structure, accountability, correction and personal attention, and free content does not supply those.

Which coaching businesses are genuinely at risk from free coaching?

The segment most exposed is the operator whose only advantage is being a cheaper source of the same recorded content — large impersonal batches, syllabus coverage without individual attention, and a price justified by access rather than outcomes. If a student could replace your course with a free playlist and lose nothing, free supply is a genuine threat to that model. An educator whose value is attention, correction and accountability is far less exposed.

Could free government coaching actually help independent educators?

Plausibly yes, for two reasons. First, the stated mechanism involves connecting good teachers through digital public infrastructure, which is an argument for individual educators being discoverable rather than employed by large brands. Second, when basic content becomes free and abundant, the differentiator shifts from who has the material to who can actually teach and be trusted, which favours capable individual educators over marketing budgets.

Should I lower my fees because of this announcement?

Not as a reflex. Cutting price is the correct response only if your offering genuinely competes on access to content, in which case the deeper problem is the offering rather than the price. If your teaching provides attention, structure and accountability, the better response is to make that value explicit to parents rather than to discount it. Reacting with a price cut to an announcement whose details do not yet exist risks permanently damaging your economics.

What is digital public infrastructure and why does it matter here?

Digital public infrastructure refers to shared, open digital systems built as public utilities — identity, payments and data exchange layers of the kind India has deployed at national scale. Its relevance is that the announcement framed free coaching as being built on such shared rails plus India's teachers, rather than as a single government-run institute. That framing points toward a networked model that connects educators, which is structurally closer to a marketplace than to a monopoly provider.

What should an independent educator do in the next twelve months?

Audit how much of your offering is pure information rather than accountability, name the specific outcome you are responsible for, move from competing on batch size to competing on attention density, build visible and honest proof of results, and fix discovery so students searching for your subject can find you. Then review the position again once the Ministry of Education publishes implementation details.
