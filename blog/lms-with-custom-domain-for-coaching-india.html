The reframe

An address is not
the hard part any more.

If you are searching for an LMS with a custom domain, you have already decided the important thing — that your students should arrive at your name, not somebody else's platform address — and the only question left is how much difficulty and money that decision costs. In 2026 the honest answer is: almost none of either. The connection is two DNS records, the certificate issues itself, and the site is live in minutes. The difficulty educators still describe when they talk about getting a website is a memory of a different decade.

That memory is worth naming, because it is what stops people. For most of the last ten years, an Indian educator who wanted a course website on their own domain was quoted somewhere between ₹40,000 and ₹2 lakh, told it would take four to eight weeks, and handed a WordPress installation with eleven plugins that would need maintaining forever. The quote was not dishonest — that genuinely was the work. But it created a belief that owning your address is an advanced, expensive, late-stage move. It is not. It is now the cheapest part of the exercise.

So this guide reframes the question. Not how do I get a website on my own domain — that is solved, and the mechanics take one section to explain. The real questions are: what has to work behind that address before it is worth sending a paying student to it; which providers give you those things and at what price; and what an owned address actually buys you, as opposed to what educators hope it buys them. The last one is where most of the disappointment lives, and it is the one nobody selling you a website will raise.

Across the educators we work with, the pattern is consistent: the domain is never the reason a coaching site fails. It fails because there was nothing behind the address worth paying for, or because nobody knew the address existed.

One clarification before the mechanics, because it decides how you read everything below. A learning management system and a website builder solve different problems that look identical from outside. A website builder produces pages. An LMS produces enrolments, entitlements and results — it knows which student paid for which batch, which lecture they may open, what they scored, and what happens when their access expires. You can build a handsome coaching website on a page builder and still have no way to stop a ₹12,000 course being forwarded to a WhatsApp group of two hundred. That distinction is the whole subject of the nine layers.

The shift

What actually changed —
the LMS became the website.

The change that made this easy was architectural, not cosmetic. Course platforms used to be a walled application you sent students into — a login page with a vendor's name on it, hanging off the side of a marketing website you maintained separately. The marketing site was yours; the classroom was theirs; the two were joined by a link and a prayer. Educators paid twice, maintained two systems, and lost students in the gap between them.

What replaced it is a single system that renders both the public storefront and the private classroom under one address. The pages a prospective student browses — your subjects, your batches, your fees, your credentials — and the pages a paid student opens — the lecture, the PDF, the mock test, the score — are the same application, differing only by who is logged in. Once that is true, the domain stops being a website-shaped problem and becomes a routing problem, which is a problem computers solved in the 1980s.

Three things had to arrive together for this to work at Indian price points, and they did. Free automated certificates removed the last recurring cost and the last manual step from HTTPS. Edge routing that can serve thousands of separate domains from shared infrastructure made per-educator custom domains cheap to operate rather than a bespoke deployment each time. And UPI made checkout on an individual educator's site feel as safe to an Indian student as checkout on a large marketplace — a trust problem, not a technical one, and arguably the hardest of the three.

Reframe

You are not buying a website. You are choosing where your students' records live.

The address is a five-minute decision. The system behind it holds every enrolment, every payment, every test score and every phone number you will ever collect — and the cost of moving that later is the only lock-in that has ever mattered.

This is also why "get a website made" has become bad advice for most educators. A brochure site built by a freelancer for ₹25,000 will look good and do nothing: it cannot take a payment, gate a lecture, score a test, or tell you who watched what. Six months later you buy an LMS anyway, and the brochure becomes a second thing to maintain. If you are weighing that fork, the longer argument is in running an online coaching academy without coding, and the page-level craft is in how to create a landing page for an online course.

Question Often Asked

Can I just use a website builder plus Google Drive instead of an LMS?

You can, and for a first ₹500 note set it is a reasonable way to test demand without spending anything. It stops working the moment the price rises or the batch grows. A Drive link has no concept of who paid, no expiry, no per-student access, and no way to revoke — so the first student who shares it has effectively made your product free, and you will not know it happened. The practical threshold is around the point where a single item costs more than ₹1,000 or a batch crosses about thirty students. Past that, you are not saving money by avoiding an LMS; you are financing your own leakage.

The mechanics

How connecting your own domain
really works.

Connecting a custom domain to an LMS is a directory update, not a migration. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is copied, and no files move. You are editing the internet's phone book so that requests for yourinstitute.com are answered by the servers already running your academy. That is why it takes minutes rather than weeks, and why it can be undone just as fast if you change your mind.

Two records do all of it, and it is worth understanding what each one is for rather than pasting them blind.

1

Record one

A TXT record that proves the domain is yours

The platform generates a short unique string and asks you to publish it in your domain's DNS. Only someone with access to the registrar account can do that, so seeing the string is proof of control. It stops anyone from claiming a domain they do not own — including, importantly, someone claiming yours.

2

Record two

A CNAME record that points the domain at the platform

This is the record that actually routes students. It makes your name an alias for the platform's address, so every request for your site is answered by the infrastructure serving your academy. On the bare apex form of a domain some registrars need an A record or an ALIAS-style record instead — a DNS standards quirk, not a platform limitation.

After that, the work is automatic. The platform re-checks DNS every few minutes; the moment both records resolve, it enables the domain and requests the HTTPS certificate itself, then renews it on its own schedule from then on. You are never asked to buy, install or remember a certificate. In practice this whole sequence finishes in minutes.

Why automatic renewal stopped being optional in 2026. Certificate lifetimes are collapsing across the industry, and Let's Encrypt — which underpins a very large share of the web's free HTTPS — switched its main issuance profile to 45-day certificates in May 2026, down from 90[1]. A stack that requires a human to renew now requires that human eight times a year, forever. Miss once and every student sees a browser security warning instead of your class.

Two practical details save most of the support tickets. First, enter the domain without the https prefix and without a trailing slash — the field wants the name, not the URL. Second, lower the TTL on your DNS records to around 300 seconds before you make the change. TTL is how long other networks are allowed to cache the old answer; leaving it at a default of 24 hours is the single most common reason a correctly configured domain appears not to work yet.

Question Often Asked

How long does a custom domain actually take to go live?

Usually a few minutes, and the delay is almost never the platform. Verification and certificate issuance are automatic once both records resolve. What varies is DNS propagation — the time for your change to reach every network — which is typically under an hour and can stretch to 24 to 48 hours in the worst case, depending on the TTL that was in place before you edited and how aggressively intermediate resolvers cache. If nothing has happened after an hour, the cause is almost always a typo in the pasted record, a record added to the wrong domain, or a registrar that silently appends the domain name to the host field, producing yourinstitute.com.yourinstitute.com.

One more thing worth knowing before you start: on AllCoaching your free address keeps working. Every academy is published at yourname.allcoaching.in the moment it is created, and that address continues to resolve after the custom domain is connected. Every link you already sent — the WhatsApp forward, the Telegram post, the number printed on a pamphlet — keeps working instead of quietly becoming a dead end the day you upgrade.

The sequencing

Free subdomain vs your own domain —
when each is right.

A free subdomain and a custom domain are not a cheap option and a good option. They are the right answer at two different moments, and the cost of getting the order wrong is larger than the cost of the domain. The subdomain's job is to let you find out whether anyone will pay. The custom domain's job is to make sure the reputation you then build accrues to you.

The common sequence

Buy a domain. Wait for a site to be built. Argue about the logo. Discover in week six that the fee page is wrong. Launch to nobody. Conclude that online teaching does not work.

The sequence that works

Publish on the free address the same day. Sell one batch. Learn what students actually ask before paying. Then buy the domain, connect it in minutes, and point everything at a page you already know converts.

The argument for starting free is not thrift, it is information. The first version of your fee structure, your batch names and your course description is almost always wrong, and you find out by selling, not by planning. An address you can publish in an afternoon lets you be wrong cheaply. An address that arrives at the end of a six-week build makes being wrong expensive enough that most educators defend the mistake instead of fixing it.

The argument for moving to your own domain is equally concrete, and it is about accrual. Everything the site earns — search rankings, backlinks, the pamphlets in circulation, the number students have saved, the mentions that AI assistants read when deciding who to recommend — attaches to whatever address it appeared at. On a platform subdomain, part of that value is attached to an address you do not control. On your own domain, all of it is yours, and it survives any decision you later make about providers. Move once, early, before there is much to move.

The invisible layer

The security you cannot see
from the address bar.

A padlock in the address bar tells a student exactly one thing: that the connection between their phone and the server is encrypted. It says nothing about whether the lecture behind it can be downloaded, whether the PDF link works for someone who never paid, whether your student list is exportable by you, or whether anyone would notice if it were exported by someone else. HTTPS is the floor, not the building — and on a site collecting fees and minors' details, the difference matters legally as well as commercially.

Six questions separate a secure setup from a decorated one. Ask them of any provider, including us, and treat a vague answer as an answer.

1

Transport

Is the certificate issued and renewed without me?

With certificate lifetimes now as short as 45 days[1], any arrangement where a human remembers to renew is a scheduled outage. The correct answer is that the platform issues and renews it automatically and you never see it.

2

Entitlement

Is access bound to a logged-in student, or to a link?

This is the difference between a business and a leak. If a lecture or note set opens for anyone holding the URL, you are not selling access — you are selling the first copy. Access should resolve against the student account that paid, and end when their entitlement does.

3

Delivery

Are video and PDF served through the platform, or from a public folder?

Content on an unlisted YouTube link or an open Drive folder is public content with an obscure address. Serving through the platform means the file is fetched for an authenticated session rather than handed over permanently. Deeper treatment sits in secure video hosting for educational content.

4

Money

Do card details ever touch anything I control?

They should not, ever. Checkout should be handled by a regulated gateway so that card data never reaches your site, your laptop or your WhatsApp. This is also why collecting fees on a personal UPI ID stops being viable the moment you need receipts, refunds and a record — see the best UPI payment gateway for online courses.

5

Student data

Can I export everything, and can a student have their data deleted?

Both matter. Export is your exit route; deletion is an obligation under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023, and your students are frequently minors. A provider who cannot demonstrate either is asking you to carry a legal risk on their behalf. Context in Indian EdTech laws and regulations for teachers.

6

Recovery

Who answers on a Sunday when 200 students cannot log in?

Backups, uptime and a reachable human are a security property, because an outage during an admission window costs the same as a breach and happens far more often. Ask what the support channel is and what the response window is before you need it.

Be precise about what any platform actually promises. Encrypted delivery, gated access and platform-served files are ordinary hygiene. Hard anti-piracy — device-locked playback, forensic watermarking, licensed DRM — is a separate and heavier layer, and any vendor implying the first is the second is telling you something that will be tested by your first popular course. The realistic version of that argument is in video DRM protection for Indian course creators and protecting course content from piracy for free.

On AllCoaching, the parts of that list the platform is responsible for are structural rather than optional: the HTTPS certificate on your custom domain is issued and renewed by the platform, content is served to authenticated students rather than as open links, checkout runs through the platform's payment rails so card data never reaches you, and student and payment records live in your studio in exportable form. What we do not claim is a hard DRM layer, because claiming it would be the kind of thing you only discover was untrue after it mattered.

The compounding asset

Why an owned domain compounds —
in Google and in AI answers.

An owned domain is the only part of an educator's online presence that appreciates. Rankings, links, citations, saved bookmarks, printed pamphlets, the address a former student gives a junior — all of it attaches to whatever name it appeared under. On a platform address, part of that accrual belongs to the platform. On your own domain, all of it is yours, and it survives every future decision you make about software. That is the entire case for a custom domain, and it is enough on its own.

What has changed since 2024 is where the accrual pays off. Students increasingly ask an assistant rather than a search box — who is the best UPSC economy teacher in Hindi, where can I buy a CAT quant test series — and the systems answering those questions decide who to name by assembling an entity from consistent mentions across the web. A single stable domain that carries your name, your subjects, your faculty page and your fees is a far cleaner entity to assemble than a scatter of platform profiles. This is the practical reason a domain now matters more than it did, and the mechanics are covered in SEO strategies for online course creators.

Question Often Asked

Will moving from a platform subdomain to my own domain hurt my Google rankings?

Not if the move is done the way Google itself documents. Its site-move guidance is to redirect every old URL to its exact new equivalent, keep those redirects in place for as long as possible and at minimum a year, and submit a Change of Address in Search Console for every verified variant of the old address, including www and non-www forms[8]. Done that way, ranking signals transfer rather than reset. There is a rankings dip in the weeks around any move, which is precisely why moving early — while the old address has little accumulated authority to lose — is the cheapest possible time to do it. Waiting three years to buy a domain does not avoid the cost; it just makes it larger.

Two mistakes waste an owned domain more reliably than anything Google does. The first is running the same content at two addresses at once — an old brochure site and a new academy, both live, both indexed, competing with each other. Pick one, redirect the other. The second is treating the domain as the marketing plan. A domain is a place for attention to land. It does not create attention, and no amount of technical polish converts a page nobody has reached.

The playbook

The 7-step launch —
registrar to first sale.

Buy the domain in your own name

Register it yourself at GoDaddy, Namecheap, Hostinger or any registrar for roughly ₹600–1,200 a year. Use your own email and your own payment method, keep auto-renew on, and never let an agency or a relative hold the account. Whoever controls the registrar login controls the address — not whoever paid for it.

3

Step 03

Enter the domain in your studio, without the https prefix

Open the website settings and type the domain exactly as you own it — no https, no trailing slash. The platform generates the two records. Connecting your own domain is a one-time ₹10,000 per domain, so settle that first — selling itself stays free whether or not you ever connect one.

4

Step 04

Paste the TXT and CNAME records at your registrar

Open the DNS panel and add both records exactly as shown. Copy and paste rather than retyping, and set a low TTL such as 300 seconds. If your registrar appends the domain name to the host field automatically, watch for the doubled-name mistake — it is the most common failure of the whole process.

5

Step 05

Let verification and HTTPS complete by themselves

The platform re-checks every few minutes, turns the domain on once both records resolve, and issues the certificate automatically — renewing it on its own from then on. Usually minutes; DNS can take up to 24–48 hours to reach every network in the worst case. Your free address keeps working throughout.

6

Step 06

Load real content before you announce the address

At minimum: one complete course, one PDF note set and one test series behind the checkout, plus a template, a logo and a plain paragraph saying who you teach and for which exam. A live domain with an empty catalogue converts nothing, and a first impression on a paid site is expensive to repair.

7

Step 07

Run one real purchase end to end before telling anyone

Buy one of your own items for a small amount, from a phone not logged in as the educator. Confirm the payment completes, the receipt arrives, access unlocks immediately, the video and PDF open, and the sale appears in your studio with the payout recorded. Only then send the link to students.

Realistic timeline. Steps 3 to 5 — the actual domain connection — take minutes. Step 2 depends on how long you take to choose a name. Step 6 is the real project, and for most educators it is a weekend. Total elapsed time for someone with content ready: one to two days, of which the technical portion is under ten minutes.

The failure modes

Six mistakes that make
a beautiful site earn nothing.

None of these are technical failures. Every one of them is a decision made early, for a sensible-sounding reason, that becomes expensive later. They are listed in the order they usually happen.

Mistake 01

Letting someone else register the domain

The agency does it, or a cousin who is good with computers, and the account sits under their email with their card on file. Two years later the relationship ends or the card expires, and the address your entire student base knows is controlled by someone who no longer answers your calls. Register it yourself, keep auto-renew on, and store the login where you can find it.

Mistake 02

Buying the domain before the content exists

The domain becomes the project. Weeks go into a logo and a colour, and no batch is sold. The purchase feels like progress precisely because it is the only step that can be completed without teaching anything. Publish free, sell one batch, then buy the name.

Mistake 03

Choosing a name that borrows someone else's

A near-copy of a large institute's name looks like a shortcut to credibility and is a liability with a delay fuse. It invites a legal notice, it confuses the students you do reach, and it makes you permanently unrankable against the brand you imitated. An ordinary name you own outright beats a clever name you are borrowing.

Mistake 04

Launching an empty catalogue

The domain resolves, the design is good, and there is one course listed as "coming soon". Every visitor you spent effort attracting arrives, finds nothing purchasable, and does not come back. Attention is the scarcest thing you have and it does not queue. Do not announce until at least one complete item can be bought.

Mistake 05

Assuming the domain brings traffic

This is the expensive one. A new domain has no visitors, no rankings and no history — it is a shop on a street nobody walks down yet. Educators consistently expect a measurable lift on the day they switch addresses, and there is none. The domain changes who owns the door, not how many people walk through it.

Mistake 06

Never running a real test purchase

The first person to discover the checkout is broken should not be a paying student. Buy your own item at a small price from a logged-out phone and follow it through to the payout record. Ten minutes here prevents the specific failure that costs both the sale and the reputation.

The honest limit

The part a website
will never solve.

Everything above is true and none of it produces a student. A domain, an LMS, a certificate, a template and a checkout together give you a shop that works perfectly. What they do not give you is anyone walking past it. This is the constraint that survives every technology decision an educator makes, and it is the reason so many well-built coaching sites sit at a handful of visits a month while their owners conclude that online teaching does not work.

The reason is structural rather than personal. A standalone website has no network effect. Every visitor must be brought to it individually, by you, at your cost — a reel, an ad, a WhatsApp forward, a hoarding. Nothing about another educator succeeding makes your site easier to find, and nothing about your hundredth student makes your hundred-and-first cheaper to acquire. The cost of attention rises with competition and never falls with scale, which is precisely backwards from how a business should behave.

"Content is king, but distribution is queen and she wears the pants."

— Jonathan Perelman, formerly of BuzzFeed

A marketplace inverts that arithmetic, and it is the only structure that does. When students arrive to browse rather than to visit one known name, every educator on the platform is discoverable by people who did not come looking for them — and each additional educator makes the destination more worth visiting, which makes discovery cheaper for everyone already there. That is a compounding asset; an advertising budget is a consumable. This is why AllCoaching pairs the branded academy on your own domain with a shared student marketplace instead of selling only the software: the domain answers who owns this, and the marketplace answers how does anyone find it.

It is worth being exact about what this does and does not mean, because overpromising here is the standard industry move. A marketplace listing is not a guarantee of students, and it does not make a weak course sell. What it changes is the source of attention: instead of every visitor being one you paid for, some fraction arrives because the destination itself has traffic. For an educator with no existing audience that fraction is the entire difference between a live site and a business. The test

Ask any provider: where does my first visitor come from?

If the answer is a variant of "you bring them", you are buying a container. That may be exactly what you need — but price it accordingly, and add the acquisition budget to the quote before comparing it with anything else.

The verdict

The verdict.

So: an LMS on your own custom domain, in 2026, is two DNS records and a few minutes, and it costs ₹600–1,200 a year for the domain plus whatever your provider charges to point at it. The technical difficulty that kept Indian educators renting somebody else's address for a decade is gone. If you already own a domain, connect it this week — there is no argument for leaving it pointed at a dead brochure. If you do not, publish on a free address today, sell one batch, and buy the name once you know what it should say.

What has not changed is the harder half. The nine layers behind the address still have to work — particularly entitlement, delivery and money, which is where page builders quietly fail and where the difference between an LMS and a website becomes a revenue number rather than a preference. And no configuration of any of it produces a student. A domain decides who owns the door. It does not decide how many people walk through it. Any provider who lets you believe otherwise is selling you a container and calling it a business.

Across the educators we have watched launch through 2026, the ones who do this well share a short discipline:

  • They sell before they build — one batch on a free address beats six weeks of design.
  • They register the domain themselves — own email, own card, auto-renew on, no exceptions.
  • They check entitlement before aesthetics — who can open what, until when, is the layer that pays.
  • They run one real test purchase — logged out, on a phone, all the way to the payout record.
  • They budget for attention, not just software — or they choose a model that supplies some of it.
  • They compute cost at their own revenue — at year one and again at year three, not in the abstract.

Do that and the decision stops being frightening. It becomes what it actually is: a cheap, reversible, ten-minute technical step, wrapped around the only question that ever mattered — whether enough people know your teaching exists. Glossary

Glossary —
key terms.

Term

Custom Domain

A web address you register and control yourself, such as yourinstitute.com, pointed at a platform that serves your academy. Distinct from a branded theme: branding changes what the page looks like, a custom domain changes who owns the address the page lives at.

Term

Subdomain

An address issued under a parent domain, such as yourname.allcoaching.in. It is free, instant and fully functional for selling, but the parent domain belongs to the platform, so the address itself is borrowed rather than owned.

Term

DNS

The Domain Name System, the internet's directory that translates a human-readable name into the server that answers for it. Connecting a domain to a platform is entirely a DNS exercise; no files move anywhere.

Term

CNAME Record

A DNS entry that makes one name an alias for another, so requests for your domain are answered by the platform's servers. It is the record that actually routes your students, and it works on subdomains such as www but not on every apex domain.

Term

TXT Verification Record

A short string the platform asks you to publish in your domain's DNS to prove you control it. It exists so nobody can claim a domain they do not own; once seen by the platform it has done its job and simply stays in place.

Term

Apex Domain

The bare form of a domain with no prefix, such as example.com rather than www.example.com. It matters in practice because the DNS standard restricts CNAME records at the apex, which is why some registrars require an A record or an ALIAS-style record there instead.

Term

TLS Certificate

The credential that turns http into https and puts the padlock in the address bar. Modern certificates are short-lived and issued automatically, which makes automatic renewal a requirement rather than a convenience: an unrenewed certificate takes a paid course site offline for every student at once.

Term

DNS Propagation

The delay between changing a DNS record and every network worldwide seeing the change, caused by caching at intermediate resolvers. Lowering the TTL before a change shortens it; it is the single most common reason a correctly configured domain appears not to work yet.

Term

Learning Management System

Software that hosts courses, delivers video and notes, runs assessments, tracks progress and controls who can access what. Distinct from a website builder, which produces pages: an LMS produces enrolments, entitlements and results.

Term

White-Label Academy

A platform-run academy presented entirely under the educator's own name, logo and domain, with the vendor invisible to students. It removes the vendor's branding but not the vendor's role, which is why data export and exit terms matter more than the logo does.

What is an LMS with a custom domain?

It is a learning management system — course hosting, video, notes, test series, student logins and checkout — served to your students at a web address you own, such as yourinstitute.com, instead of a platform address. The software still runs on the provider's infrastructure. What changes is the name on the door: students see only your brand, links you share carry your name, and the search authority the site earns accumulates against an address that stays yours if you ever move providers.

Do I need my own domain to start selling courses online in India?

No, and buying one first is a common way to waste two weeks. On AllCoaching every academy is published at a free address of the form yourname.allcoaching.in from the moment it is created, and that page can take real payments the same day. A domain is worth buying once there is something on the page worth visiting. Sell first, then upgrade the address.

How do I connect my own domain to my coaching website?

Two DNS records do the whole job. You type your domain into the studio without the https prefix, and the platform generates a TXT record that proves the domain is yours and a CNAME record that points it at the platform. You paste both into the DNS panel at your registrar — GoDaddy, Namecheap, Hostinger or whoever you bought from. The platform re-checks every few minutes, turns the domain on by itself, and issues the HTTPS certificate automatically.

How long does it take for a custom domain to go live?

Usually a few minutes. Once both DNS records resolve, verification and certificate issuance complete without anyone touching anything. The variable is not the platform but DNS itself: changes can take up to 24 to 48 hours to reach every network in the worst case, and some registrars cache aggressively. Setting a low TTL, such as 300 seconds, before you make the change shortens that tail considerably.

Does AllCoaching sell domains?

No. You buy the domain yourself from any registrar and connect it, which is deliberate. A domain registered in your own account, with your own email and payment method, is one of the few genuinely portable assets an educator owns. If the platform held the registration, leaving would mean leaving the address behind — and that is exactly the dependency this feature exists to remove.

Does my free allcoaching.in address stop working after I connect my own domain?

No. Both keep working. The free yourname.allcoaching.in address continues to resolve alongside the custom domain, which matters more than it sounds: every WhatsApp forward, Telegram post and printed poster carrying the old link keeps working, so nothing you shared in your first months quietly turns into a dead link the day you upgrade.

What does a custom domain cost in India in 2026?

The domain itself is cheap — roughly ₹600 to ₹1,200 a year for a .in or .com at mainstream registrars, with renewal usually costing more than the discounted first year. The expensive part is what the domain points at. Course platforms gate custom domains behind monthly plans that run from roughly ₹3,500 a month to well over ₹16,000, and a self-hosted WordPress stack adds hosting, plugin licences and maintenance on top. AllCoaching charges a one-time ₹10,000 per domain instead of a monthly plan, and selling courses stays free either way.

Selling courses is free. ₹0 to start, no subscription, and a flat 10% only on what you sell, so you keep 90%. The free web address is included with every academy and never expires. Is a coaching website on my own domain secure enough to sell paid courses?

It depends entirely on what sits behind the address, not on the address. The questions worth asking any provider are whether HTTPS is issued and renewed automatically, whether lecture and PDF access is bound to a logged-in student rather than a shareable link, whether card details ever touch the seller's own systems, and whether student data can be exported and deleted on request under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023. A domain with a padlock and an open Drive folder behind it is not secure.

Will moving to my own domain hurt my Google rankings?

Only if it is done carelessly. Google's own site-move guidance is to redirect old URLs to their new equivalents, keep those redirects in place for at least a year, and file a Change of Address in Search Console for every verified variant of the old address. Done that way, ranking signals transfer. Moving early, while the old address has little accumulated authority to lose, is by far the cheapest time to do it.
