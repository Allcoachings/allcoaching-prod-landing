The reframe

Two subscriptions,
not two models.

AppX vs Classplus is a choice between two recurring software subscriptions, not between buying an app and renting one — both charge an ongoing fee to keep your branded app and website running, both are sold through a demo call, and neither publishes its price on its own website. Both give an Indian coaching educator a branded app through which to teach, host content and collect fees. An educator comparing them is comparing two quotes they have not yet seen for a broadly similar capability.

That matters because a lot of what has been written about this comparison — including an earlier version of this very article — framed AppX as a one-time purchase and Classplus as a subscription. As of October 2026, AppX's own website describes the product as a "Software as a Service (SaaS) solution" with "maintenance and ongoing support"[1], which is the language of a recurring service, not a one-off sale. Whatever an individual quote looks like, the structure an educator should plan for is the same on both sides: you pay, and then you renew.

Once that is clear, the comparison gets simpler and more useful. The questions that decide the outcome are not "asset or service" but three practical ones: what will my total bill be each year, what do I keep if I stop paying, and where will my students come from? This guide works through all three — fairly for both vendors — and then sets out where AllCoaching fits, since we are a third option in the same decision. If the pattern of paying for an app and waiting for downloads sounds familiar, the diagnosis sits in what to do when nobody downloads your coaching app.

The structural similarity

Same billing model,
different packaging.

AppX and Classplus share the same commercial skeleton: a recurring fee, a sales-led onboarding, and a price you learn only after a conversation. Neither is unusual — this is how most enterprise software in India is sold — but it changes how an educator should compare them. When two products share a billing model, the billing model stops being the differentiator, and the details inside each quote become everything.

Those details are where the two can genuinely differ: the size of the annual fee, whether there is a separate setup charge, whether a percentage of your course sales is taken, how SMS or WhatsApp credits are billed, which features sit in which plan, and what happens to your app at the end of a term you don't renew. None of these are visible on either vendor's website, which means an educator cannot compare the two without sitting through two demos and extracting two written quotes.

The clarifying question is not "which billing model suits me". They have the same one. It is "which written quote, over three years, costs less for the same result — and which leaves me freer to leave?" That question can only be answered with numbers you collect yourself.

Question Often Asked

Isn't AppX a one-time payment for your own app?

That is how AppX was often described in older comparisons, but it is not how AppX describes itself today. As of October 2026, AppX's website calls its offering a "Software as a Service (SaaS) solution for Creators" and lists "maintenance and ongoing support" as part of what it provides, and it publishes no price or plan table — pricing is discussed on a demo call. A SaaS product with ongoing maintenance is, by definition, a recurring service: someone has to keep the app running as Android versions and Google Play policies change, and that work is paid for over time. The safe assumption for any educator is to ask AppX directly what the annual or renewal charge is, what it covers, and what happens to the app if it lapses — and to get the answer in writing before comparing it with anything else.

Option one

What AppX
actually is.

AppX is an Indian app-building company for educators and creators that sells a branded app and website under the educator's own name, delivered as a SaaS service with maintenance and ongoing support[1]. Its site positions it as a "technology partner for educators and creators", and onboarding begins with a free personalised demo. It publishes no price, no plan table and no transaction-fee schedule on its own website, so every commercial term reaches the educator through a sales conversation.

What this buys, in practice, is the usual capability set an educator needs — course and batch delivery, content hosting, live classes, tests and payment collection — inside an app that carries your brand. The genuine strength should be stated plainly: a done-for-you build with a team that handles the technical side is real value for an educator who never wants to think about software, and an app on Google Play under your own name is a credible asset when you already have an audience to send to it.

The trade-offs are the ones every sales-led subscription carries. You cannot know the cost before the call, the quote you receive is partly a function of negotiation, and the ongoing fee continues whether or not the app is earning. The pattern of paying for an app and then not earning is common enough to have its own diagnosis in paying for a coaching app but not earning.

Option two

What Classplus
actually is.

Classplus is the most established name in Indian coaching software, and like AppX its pricing is quote-based and not published on its own website. Third-party software listings report annual subscriptions in a broad band of roughly ₹8,000 to ₹50,000 or more per year depending on plan, with a setup fee charged separately and a commission on paid course sales in many contracts[2]. These are third-party figures, not Classplus's own, and a limited free tier described as Classplus Lite is reported to exclude the branded app.

The strengths deserve fair statement. Classplus is mature, widely deployed across Indian coaching institutes, and comes with the operational depth a long-established product accumulates — onboarding support, a broad feature set, and familiarity that makes it an easy internal sell. For an institute that wants a known quantity with someone to call, that is worth real money, and pretending otherwise would be unserious.

The structural point to hold onto is that quote-based pricing means the number you are given reflects the negotiation, not a published rate. It places the burden on the educator to ask precisely — especially about the setup fee and the percentage of sales, the two components most often discovered after the annual figure has anchored the conversation. The fine print

The renewal question
nobody asks.

Every subscription eventually reaches a renewal, and what happens at that moment decides how much of your business you actually own. An educator on any coaching app — AppX, Classplus or AllCoaching — should know, before signing, what happens to the app, the website, the content and the student list if they stop paying. The answer is rarely on a pricing page and almost never in a demo.

Three questions separate real ownership from the marketing version. Can you export your complete student and payment records in a usable format, on demand? Do you hold your students' contact details yourself, or does the platform mediate every message? If you don't renew, what keeps working — and for how long can you still take your content out? An educator who can answer all three confidently is independent; one who cannot is negotiating from weakness at every renewal.

The app is the least portable thing you will pay for and the most talked about. The student list is the most portable and the least discussed. Optimise for the second.

For the record, here is our own answer, so it can be compared: on AllCoaching, a non-renewed academy goes offline to students, but your courses, student list and data stay exportable. Upgrades apply on payment with the unexpired value credited, and there is no lock-in contract beyond the year you paid for. The omissions

The costs nobody
puts in the quote.

Both quotes describe a platform. Neither describes a business. Four categories of cost sit outside the subscription number, and together they routinely exceed it.

Setup, add-ons and credits

Third-party reports for Classplus mention a separate setup fee and SMS or WhatsApp credits billed on top. Ask both vendors for every line item — setup, add-on modules, messaging credits, extra storage — not just the annual figure.

Share of your sales

A percentage of course sales is reported in many Classplus contracts; AppX publishes no transaction-fee terms. A percentage grows with your success, so it must be priced at your projected revenue, not ignored because the annual fee looked small.

Marketing — usually the largest line of all

An app with no audience earns nothing. Whatever an educator spends on ads, reels and promotion to make students aware they exist typically dwarfs the platform fee — which is why comparing platform prices alone answers the smaller question.

Your own time

Demo calls, negotiation, waiting on a vendor's team for changes, and chasing support tickets are hours not spent teaching. It never appears in any quote, and for a solo educator it is often the most expensive input of all.

Across the educators who moved their academy to AllCoaching from other apps in 2026, the pattern we hear most is the same: the subscription was rarely what made online teaching unprofitable. The marketing needed to fill an app nobody could find was. That is not a criticism of either vendor — it is a description of what buying software does and does not solve.

The buying experience

Price behind a
demo call.

The least discussed difference between coaching platforms in 2026 is not a feature — it is whether you can see the price and start on your own, or have to book a call to find out either. AppX and Classplus both lead with a demo. That is a legitimate way to sell, and some educators genuinely prefer to be walked through a product by a person. But it has three costs for the buyer that are worth naming.

First, you cannot compare before you commit time: two demos, two follow-ups and two negotiations just to learn two numbers. Second, the quote is shaped by the conversation, so an educator who negotiates less pays more for the same product. Third, setup depends on the vendor's team — the academy goes live when they finish, and every later change runs through them.

The alternative is self-serve: a published price, a free trial you start yourself, and a studio where you build the academy without waiting for anyone. That is the model AllCoaching is built on — sign up, drag in your courses, tests and live classes, add your logo and domain, publish your Google Play app, and start selling, with no sales call and no demo to book. Some educators will still prefer a managed service, and that is a fair choice; the point is that it should be a choice, not the only door.

Question Often Asked

Why don't AppX and Classplus show their prices online?

Quote-based pricing is standard for sales-led software: it lets a vendor tailor packages, bundle add-ons and price according to the size of the customer and the negotiation. It is not a sign that anything is wrong. The practical consequence for an educator is that the published-price comparison simply isn't available — you have to book demos, request written quotes covering subscription, setup, commission and renewal terms, and then compare those documents side by side. The shared blind spot

The gap both
leave open.

The constraint neither subscription touches is this: both AppX and Classplus sell the software you teach through, and software has not been the scarce resource for an Indian educator for years. What remains scarce is a student who does not yet know you exist deciding to learn from you. Every step of that — being heard of, being trusted, being downloaded, being paid — is still the educator's own work under either vendor.

That is why so many well-built coaching apps sit at low download counts, a pattern examined in why your coaching app is not getting students. A standalone branded app has no network effect — one educator's students do not make another educator's app more discoverable, so each educator starts from their own followers and stays there unless they pay to grow.

Question Often Asked

I already have a YouTube channel and WhatsApp groups — doesn't that solve discovery for me?

Partly, and if you have an engaged audience you are in a far stronger position than most buyers in this category — an existing audience is the best predictor of whether any of these platforms pays for itself. But it converts people who already know you; it does not reach people who do not. A YouTube channel grows through a recommendation algorithm you do not control, and WhatsApp groups only contain students you have already met. The practical test is to ask where your last twenty paid students came from: if nearly all were existing followers, your growth ceiling is your current audience, and the subscription you choose will not raise it. That is when a marketplace, search visibility or paid ads becomes the real decision — and the app is simply where those students land once something else has found them.

The myths

Three myths in
this decision.

Three beliefs distort this choice more than any pricing detail.

Myth

"AppX is one-time, Classplus is a subscription." The framing behind most comparisons of the two.

Reality

Both are recurring services. AppX describes itself as SaaS with ongoing maintenance; the question is the size of each renewal, not whether there is one.

The second myth is "once I have my own app, students will download it" — an app serves students you have already persuaded, and discovery happens before the download. The third is "a custom quote means a better deal". A custom quote means a negotiated one; it can be better or worse than a published price, and you only know which after you have collected both. Compare on the four-line bill at your revenue, on renewal and exit terms, and on where students come from — the three things that actually move the result.

The verdict

The verdict.

The honest verdict on AppX versus Classplus is that they are two versions of the same deal — a recurring, sales-led subscription for a branded coaching app — and the better one for you is whichever written quote costs less over three years and leaves you freer to leave. AppX sells a managed, done-for-you build described on its own site as SaaS with ongoing maintenance. Classplus sells maturity and a known quantity, reported by third-party listings at roughly ₹8,000 to ₹50,000 or more a year with a setup fee and a commission. Neither shows its price before the call.

What should matter more than which invoice is smaller is that neither puts a single student in front of you. Whatever you pay, the work of being found remains yours, funded by you, and it is almost always the largest line in the whole exercise.

The educators we see making this decision well in 2026 share a short discipline:

  • They treat every option as a subscription — and ask for the year-two price, not just year one.
  • They get setup fees and sale shares in writing — the two lines discovered late most often.
  • They compute the four-line bill — at their own projected revenue, before signing.
  • They ask where the first 100 students come from — and reject any answer that is really "somehow".
  • They secure export terms early — while they still have leverage.
  • They build on a free trial first — before committing money to any vendor.

Do that, and the choice usually makes itself. If you would rather see the price, build the academy yourself and be listed where students are already looking, the self-serve route is worth trying alongside any quote you are holding. Glossary

Glossary —
key terms.

Term

White-Label App

An application built on one company's platform and published under another's branding, so students see the educator's name and logo rather than the vendor's. Distinct from a marketplace listing, where the educator appears inside a shared platform alongside other educators.

Term

SaaS Subscription

Software as a Service: software the vendor hosts, maintains and updates in return for a recurring fee. You keep using it for as long as you keep paying, which is why the renewal price and exit terms matter as much as the first-year figure.

Term

Quote-Based Pricing

A pricing approach where the vendor publishes no fixed rate and gives each customer a price after a sales conversation. Two similar buyers can pay different amounts, so comparison requires collecting written quotes rather than reading a pricing page.

Term

Setup Fee

A one-off onboarding charge levied at the start of a contract, separate from the recurring subscription. It matters most in year one, and is the figure most often omitted when educators compare annual prices.

Term

Platform Fee

A percentage of each course sale kept by the platform, also called a commission or revenue share. It grows with the educator's sales, so it has to be priced at projected revenue alongside any fixed annual fee.

Term

Total Cost of Ownership

The full cost of a platform over a period, including subscription, setup, platform fee, add-ons, hosting, payment charges and marketing. It is the only fair basis for comparing two quotes, since headline prices leave different things out.

Term

Self-Serve Onboarding

A way of starting with software where the buyer sees the price, signs up and sets everything up themselves, without a sales call or demo. Distinct from sales-led onboarding, where pricing and setup run through the vendor's team.

Term

Discovery

The mechanism by which a student who does not already know an educator comes to find them. It is supplied by search, recommendation or a marketplace — not by an app, which can only serve students who have already been persuaded to download it.

FAQ

Frequently asked
questions.

What is the main difference between AppX and Classplus?

Less than most comparisons suggest. Both are recurring, sales-led subscriptions for a branded coaching app, and neither publishes its price on its own website. AppX describes itself as a SaaS product with maintenance and ongoing support and focuses on a managed build. Classplus is the more established platform, reported by third-party listings at roughly ₹8,000 to ₹50,000 or more a year with a setup fee and a commission on sales. The real differences sit inside each written quote.

Is AppX a one-time payment?

Not according to how AppX describes itself today. As of October 2026, AppX's website calls its product a "Software as a Service (SaaS) solution" with "maintenance and ongoing support", and it publishes no prices. A SaaS product with ongoing maintenance is a recurring service. Ask AppX directly for the annual or renewal charge and what happens to the app if it lapses, and get it in writing.

Which is cheaper, AppX or Classplus?

It cannot be answered from their websites, because neither publishes a price. The only fair comparison is two written quotes priced at your own projected revenue, covering the subscription, any setup fee, any share of sales and the renewal price for year two. Add the marketing you will need, which is usually the largest cost of all and is the same under either vendor.

Does AppX or Classplus bring students to my coaching?

Neither. Both supply the software an educator teaches and sells through; neither supplies student demand. Whichever you choose, students still have to come from your WhatsApp groups, your YouTube audience, referrals, or paid advertising you fund yourself. This matters more than either price, because an app with no audience produces no revenue.

What is the real Year-1 cost of a coaching app subscription?

Four lines, not one: the subscription, any setup fee, any share of your sales, and the marketing you pay for. Illustratively, a ₹30,000 subscription with a ₹20,000 setup fee and a 5% share on ₹3 lakh of sales comes to ₹65,000 before any marketing. These figures are illustrative, meant to show the mechanism rather than to quote any vendor.

Is Classplus free for small tutors?

Classplus is reported by third-party sources to offer a limited free tier, described as Classplus Lite, aimed at tutors with a small student base, which does not include the branded app or the advanced features of the paid plans. The main product is quote-based and paid. Confirm current terms directly with Classplus.

What should I ask before signing with AppX or Classplus?

Get six things in writing: the annual fee, any setup fee, any percentage taken on sales, the renewal price for year two, how student data is exported, and what happens to your app and your collected money if you stop paying. Marketing language about ownership is not the same as contractual terms, and the difference only shows at renewal or exit.

Can I move from AppX or Classplus to another platform later?

Usually yes, but how painful it is depends on terms agreed at the start. The two things that decide it are whether you can export your student and payment records in a usable format, and whether your students follow you. Ask about data export before you sign rather than when you want to leave, because that is when leverage is lowest.
