Chapter 01

The Right Dream, Sold the Wrong Way —
why "own your academy" was never the mistake.

In March 2020, when classrooms went dark, Indian educators were handed an idea: build your own academy online. Your name. Your students. Your income. Zoom calls became classrooms, WhatsApp groups became study halls, and a wave of app-building companies promised every teacher a branded app of their own.

The dream was right. An educator should own their brand, their students and their income. What went wrong was how it was sold.

Owning an academy usually meant calling a sales team, sitting through a demo, signing a quote, paying a setup fee, and then waiting weeks while somebody else built the app. Every change needed a support ticket. Every new feature had a price. The educator owned the logo on the screen, but not the controls behind it.

And once the app went live, most educators were left alone with it. No students arrived on their own. The app was a mohalle ki coaching centre that had gone digital, but stayed just as invisible, just as local, just as limited.

"The problem was never that educators wanted their own academy. The problem was that someone else held the keys to it, and nobody helped students find the door."

So the lesson of those years is not "stop building your own academy." It is this: an educator should be able to build it themselves, in minutes, without a developer, and it should not leave them alone once it is live. That is the idea this manifesto is built on.

Chapter 02

The Educator's Trap —
developer, marketer, payments desk, and teacher last.

Here is what nobody told the educators who went online: having an academy and running an academy are two completely different jobs. The companies that built their apps were paid when the app was delivered. Everything after that was the educator's problem.

So educators did what entrepreneurs do. They tried everything.

They became entertainers. Social media rewards content that is dramatic or amusing, so educators began chasing views instead of teaching depth. A chemistry teacher who spent years mastering reaction mechanisms now spent hours scripting 60-second reels. Quality of instruction fell in proportion to the pressure to perform for an algorithm.

"Every rupee spent on Instagram ads is a rupee not spent on a better curriculum. Every hour making reels is an hour not spent preparing a better class."

They spent on marketing instead of education. Without anything bringing students to them, acquisition became a paid game. A small educator with, say, ₹10,000 a month for ads was competing for the same eyeballs as companies spending crores. That was never a fair fight.

They became technically dependent. Payment gateways that failed at checkout, video hosting that cost more as they grew, app updates that needed a developer, support requests that landed on the educator's own phone. Every teacher became an accidental startup founder, without the funding, team or tools.

They faced hidden costs at every turn. Setup fees. Feature unlocks. Transaction cuts. Annual maintenance. Charges that only appeared after signing. What started as "your own platform" carried a cost structure most educators never saw clearly until it was too late.

Pain 01

Someone else holds the keys

The academy carries your name, but every change goes through a sales rep, a developer or a support ticket. Ownership in name, dependency in practice.

Pain 02

Teaching replaced by performing

Algorithm-driven reach rewards entertainment, not instruction. The best educators aren't always the most viral, but the feed rewards virality, not pedagogy.

Pain 03

Marketing budget eating quality

Every rupee spent on paid acquisition is taken from curriculum, better tools or a co-teacher. Educators are forced to choose between growing and teaching well.

Pain 04

Technical burden

Payments, video hosting, app updates, logins. Educators end up running a tech company they never signed up for, with no tech team behind them.

Pain 05

Hidden costs and opaque cuts

Setup fees, feature unlocks, gateway cuts and renewal surprises quietly eat into the margin of every sale.

Pain 06

Alone after launch

A standalone academy is visible only to people who already know you exist. No shared discovery, no network, no new students arriving on their own.

Chapter 03

The Student's Side —
great teachers, hidden behind walls.

We spend most of our time talking about educators. But the student's side of this story is just as broken.

Consider a student in Patna preparing for SSC CGL. They have heard about a good reasoning teacher from a friend. They go looking. They find a reel, then a Telegram channel, then an app link that asks them to download, register, verify an OTP and trust a payment page they have never seen before. Then they do it again for the maths teacher. And again for the English teacher.

"The best teacher in India is invisible if students can't find them, and unconvincing if what they do find doesn't look like a real academy."

Two things decide whether that student enrols. First, can they find the teacher at all? Second, when they arrive, does it feel like a real academy they can trust: a proper website, a clear course page, a familiar way to pay, an app with the teacher's name on it?

Most independent educators fail on one or both. Not because their teaching is poor, but because what surrounds the teaching is broken. A course that a student never finds, or finds and does not trust, is not a content failure. It is an infrastructure failure.

A simple question that exposes everything: How does a student in Jaipur, looking for the best biology teacher for NEET, find a teacher who has 800 YouTube subscribers and no ad budget? Today, mostly, they don't. They end up with whoever advertised most, not whoever taught best. And if they do find the right teacher, they land on a WhatsApp number and a UPI ID instead of an academy.

Fixing this means giving every educator two things at once: a professional academy under their own name, and a way to be found by students who don't know them yet.

Chapter 04

Why Big EdTech
is not your friend.

The case for joining a giant EdTech platform sounds reasonable: reach, infrastructure, brand trust, students handed to you. Why not just join them?

Because what those platforms offer educators is not partnership. It is absorption.

Your brand disappears. Students enrol with the platform, not with you. If you leave, they keep the students, the reviews and the data. You leave with nothing you can carry.

Your content becomes their asset. The lectures, notes and question banks you created are used to sell the next batch, with or without you.

Your income is never fully yours. Revenue splits are rarely transparent, and the platform takes the larger share because it controls discovery.

Your growth is capped by their algorithm. The platform promotes what grows platform revenue. You are competing with your own platform's interests.

Chapter 05

The False Choice —
ownership or reach, never both.

For half a decade, every option in Indian EdTech asked educators to give something up. Own your academy, and accept that you are on your own. Join a platform, and accept that you are a listing on someone else's shelf.

We think that choice was never real. It came from how the tools were built, not from anything educators needed.

"Ownership and reach are not opposites. They only looked that way because nobody built one product that gave an educator both."

Look at how good shopkeepers work. A shop owner has their own signboard, their own counter, their own regular customers who come straight to them. They also sit in a market where new customers walk past every day. Nobody asks them to choose between their own shop and the market. The shop is theirs. The market brings footfall.

Educators deserve the same. Their own academy, on their own domain, under their own name, built and run by them. And alongside it, a shared marketplace where students who are looking for a teacher can find them, so that every new educator who joins makes it easier for every other educator to be discovered.

That is the layer Indian education has been missing. Not another app. Not another platform. A foundation that lets each educator build their own academy, and connects all of them.

Chapter 06

AllCoaching —
The Operating System of Education.

We describe AllCoaching as the Operating System of Education deliberately. Not an app-building agency. Not a platform that rents you a shelf. An operating system.

An operating system doesn't decide what you build on it. It gives you everything underneath, so you can build exactly what you want. That is what AllCoaching is for an educator: you build your academy, we run everything beneath it.

The AllCoaching premise is simple: any educator in India should be able to build their own LMS in a few clicks. Drag and drop courses, tests, notes and live classes. Add their logo and colours, connect their own domain, publish their own app on Google Play. Take payments by UPI or card. And be listed on the AllCoaching marketplace so new students can find them. No developer, no sales call, no demo to book.

This means two paths, from one account, and the educator chooses how far to go:

Your own academy. A white-label academy that carries your name: a website on your own domain like youracademy.in, your own app on Google Play, your logo and colours throughout. Students see your brand, not ours. You set it up yourself, change it whenever you want, and you can export your courses, students and data at any time.

The marketplace. Your courses are also listed on the AllCoaching app on Android, iOS and web, where students who are looking for a teacher can discover you. It works alongside your own academy. It does not replace it.

Our economics are tied to the educator's success. One yearly plan with a published price, and a platform fee only when a student pays, from 10% down to 5% depending on the plan. When educators sell more, we earn more. That is not marketing language. It is our business model.
