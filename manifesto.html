The AllCoaching Manifesto

Education's next revolution won't come from a bigger platform. It will come from a million independent academies.

For decades, the people who teach India have built everything except the thing they stand inside. This is our case for handing the keys back: every educator running an academy of their own, under their own name, with the technology running quietly underneath.

00Prologue

The quiet kranti.

Every few years, Indian education is promised a revolution. A new app, a new platform, a bigger brand on a bigger hoarding. And every few years, the people who actually teach end up holding a little less of what they built.

We think the real revolution has not happened yet. When it comes, it will not look like one giant company teaching everyone. It will look like the opposite. A physics teacher in a small town, a tutor who teaches in Hindi, an exam mentor with a loyal batch, a subject expert who has never written a line of code. Each of them running an academy of their own, under their own name, as professional as anything a large brand could put on a screen.

Not one platform with a million teachers on it. A million academies, each owned by the person who teaches in it.

India has an estimated 3.5 lakh independent educators who teach for a living: coaching-institute owners, home tutors, exam mentors, subject and skill teachers. Between them they hold more knowledge than any company ever will. Most of them teach on borrowed ground. Someone else's building, someone else's app, someone else's platform. They have the knowledge. What they have rarely had is the infrastructure, and the control over it.

Teaching has always been powerful in India. Owning the teaching has rarely been in the teacher's hands.

This manifesto is about changing that. It is long, because the problem is old and the change we are arguing for is large. It sets out how education came to be owned by everyone except the teacher, why the obvious fixes failed, what we believe should replace them, and what we are building at AllCoaching to make it ordinary.

We call it the Operating System of Education. You build the academy. We run everything beneath it.

We are not neutral about any of this. We are on the educator's side. If that makes this a manifesto rather than a brochure, good. A brochure describes a product. A manifesto describes the world you want, and then holds you to it.

01How education was owned

Every era moved the keys further from the teacher.

Before saying what we believe, we have to be honest about how we got here. Look at the last few decades of Indian teaching as three eras, and a pattern appears.

The building era: the walls owned the business

For a generation, a teaching career began with a room. A hall above a sweet shop, a rented floor near the railway station, a board painted by hand and a bench that seated more students than it was built for. The teacher could be brilliant, but the reach of that brilliance was set by geography: how far a student could cycle, which city had an exam culture, which family could afford a hostel in a coaching town.

Those who grew did it by renting more space, in more expensive places, under bigger hoardings. Rent rose before income did. The best location, not the best lesson, decided who was found first. A great teacher in the wrong lane stayed small. In the building era, the landlord and the location owned the business, and the teacher paid for both.

The app era: someone else held the keys

Then the classroom closed, and everything moved into a phone. Almost overnight an educator needed a website, an app, a payment page and a way to stream classes. Most did not have a developer, so they hired someone who did. Agencies arrived with quotes, demos and setup fees. They built the app, held the code, managed the store listing and billed for every change.

The educator's name was on the icon. The keys were in someone else's pocket. Want a new course page? Raise a ticket. Want to change a price? Wait for the developer. Want to leave? Start again from nothing. In the app era, the vendor owned the controls, and the teacher paid for those too.

The platform era: the teacher became supply

The third era looked like a rescue. Large EdTech platforms offered reach, polish and a ready audience. Many good teachers joined them, and some did well for a while. But slowly, the teacher's own name became smaller than the platform's. Students enrolled with the brand, not the person. Recordings, reviews and student lists stayed with the platform. Prices, promotion and visibility were decided somewhere else.

In the platform era, the platform owned the student relationship. The teacher became inventory, a face on a thumbnail inside someone else's catalogue.

Three eras, three owners: the landlord, the agency, the platform. Each brought something real. Each moved a little more power away from the person doing the teaching.

The room gave students a place to gather. The app took teaching beyond the city. The platform brought scale. We are not dismissing any of it. We are pointing at what each era cost: a little more of the educator's control, a little more of their margin, a little more of their name.

We think the fourth era turns that around. Not by going back to the room, and not by building a bigger platform, but by giving every educator the infrastructure that only large institutions used to have, and leaving the ownership with them.

What we mean by revolution

Not louder marketing. Not a bigger platform. A kranti in who owns the means of teaching: the brand, the students, the price, the data and the decisions.

When those sit with the educator, everything else in Indian education starts to rearrange itself around good teaching instead of around rent, vendors and ad budgets.

02The right dream, sold the wrong way

"Own your academy" was never the mistake.

When classrooms went dark in 2020, Indian educators were handed an idea: build your own academy online. Your name. Your students. Your income.

Video calls became classrooms, WhatsApp groups became study halls, and a wave of app-building companies promised every teacher a branded app of their own. Teachers who had never thought of themselves as business owners suddenly were.

The dream was right. An educator should own their brand, their students and their income. What went wrong was how it was sold.

Owning an academy usually meant calling a sales team, sitting through a demo, negotiating a quote, paying a setup fee, and then waiting weeks while somebody else built the thing. Every change needed a support ticket. Every new feature came with a price. The educator owned the logo on the screen, but not the controls behind it.

And once the app went live, most educators were left alone with it. No students arrived on their own. The app was a mohalle ki coaching centre that had gone digital, but stayed just as invisible, just as local, just as limited. It had moved online without gaining any of the internet's reach.

The problem was never that educators wanted their own academy. The problem was that someone else held the keys, and nobody helped students find the door.

Many educators drew the wrong lesson from those years. Some decided that owning an academy was a vanity project, that the only realistic path was to join a large platform and accept its terms. Others kept paying for an app they could not change, because starting over felt worse.

The real lesson is different. It is not "stop building your own academy." It is this: an educator should be able to build it themselves, in minutes, without a developer, and it should not leave them alone once it is live. Ownership without control is a costume. Control without reach is a locked room. The educator needs both, and needs them without asking anyone's permission.

That is the idea this manifesto is built on.

03The educator's trap

Developer, marketer, payments desk. Teacher last.

Here is what nobody told the educators who went online: having an academy and running an academy are two completely different jobs.

The companies that built their apps were paid when the app was delivered. Everything after that was the educator's problem. So educators did what entrepreneurs do. They tried everything.

They became entertainers. Social feeds reward what is dramatic or amusing, so educators began chasing views instead of teaching depth. A chemistry teacher who spent years mastering reaction mechanisms now spent evenings scripting sixty-second reels. The quality of instruction fell in proportion to the pressure to perform for an algorithm.

They spent on marketing instead of education. With nothing bringing students to them, acquisition became a paid game. A small educator with a modest monthly ad budget was bidding for the same attention as companies spending crores. That was never a fair fight, and it was never a contest of who teaches better.

Every rupee spent on ads is a rupee not spent on a better curriculum. Every hour making reels is an hour not spent preparing a better class.

They became technically dependent. Checkout pages that failed at the worst moment, video hosting that cost more as they grew, app updates that needed a developer, login problems that landed on the educator's own phone at eleven at night. Every teacher became an accidental startup founder, without the funding, the team or the tools.

They met hidden costs at every turn. Setup fees. Paid add-ons. Transaction cuts. Annual maintenance. Charges that only appeared after signing. What started as "your own platform" carried a cost structure most educators never saw clearly until it was too late to walk away.

Pain 01

Someone else holds the keys

The academy carries your name, but every change goes through a sales rep, a developer or a support ticket. Ownership in name, dependency in practice.

Pain 02

Performing replaces teaching

Algorithm-driven reach rewards entertainment, not instruction. The best educators are not always the most viral, and the feed rewards virality, not pedagogy.

Pain 03

Marketing eats quality

Every rupee spent on paid acquisition is taken from curriculum, better tools or a co-teacher. Educators are forced to choose between growing and teaching well.

Pain 04

The tech burden

Payments, video, app updates, logins. Educators end up running a technology company they never signed up for, with no technology team behind them.

Pain 05

Hidden costs, opaque cuts

Setup fees, paid add-ons, gateway cuts and renewal surprises quietly eat into the margin of every sale.

Pain 06

Alone after launch

A standalone academy is visible only to people who already know you exist. No shared discovery, no network, no new students arriving on their own.

The cost of the trap is not only money. It is attention. Teaching well needs long, unbroken hours: reading, preparing, solving, rehearsing an explanation until it lands. The trap breaks those hours into pieces and hands them to vendors, feeds and spreadsheets.

Over time it changes who succeeds. Not the most patient teacher, not the clearest explainer, but the one who can best tolerate being a part-time developer and a full-time marketer. That is a selection system that filters out exactly the people students need most. Any revolution in Indian education has to start by switching it off.

04The student's side

Great teachers, hidden behind walls.

We spend most of this manifesto talking about educators. But the student's side of the story is just as broken, and in the end it is the reason any of this matters.

Consider a student in Patna preparing for SSC CGL. A friend has mentioned a good reasoning teacher. The student goes looking. They find a reel, then a Telegram channel, then an app link that asks them to download, register, verify an OTP and trust a payment page they have never seen before. Then they do it again for the maths teacher. And again for English. Each step is a place where a nervous student, spending a family's money, can decide not to trust what they see.

The best teacher in India is invisible if students cannot find them, and unconvincing if what they find does not look like a real academy.

Two things decide whether that student enrols. First: can they find the teacher at all? Second: when they arrive, does it feel like a real academy they can trust? A proper website, a clear course page, a familiar way to pay, an app with the teacher's name on it.

Most independent educators fail on one or both. Not because their teaching is weak, but because what surrounds the teaching is broken. A course that a student never finds, or finds and does not trust, is not a content failure. It is an infrastructure failure.

A question that exposes everything

How does a student in Jaipur, looking for the best biology teacher for NEET, find a teacher with a small YouTube channel and no ad budget? Today, mostly, they don't. They end up with whoever advertised most, not whoever taught best. And if they do find the right teacher, they often land on a WhatsApp number and a UPI ID instead of an academy.

There is a quieter cost too. When discovery is bought, students learn to judge teachers by production value and follower counts. Prices drift up to pay for the ads. The family in a small town ends up paying for a marketing budget, not for better teaching.

Fixing this means giving every educator two things at once: a professional academy under their own name, and a way to be found by students who don't know them yet. One without the other fails the student as surely as it fails the teacher.

05Big EdTech is not the educator's friend

Not partnership. Absorption.

The case for joining a giant EdTech platform sounds reasonable: reach, infrastructure, brand trust, students handed to you. Why not just join?

Let us be precise about what we are arguing. This is not about any one company, and it is not about the people inside them, many of whom care deeply about students. It is about a model. A model in which the platform is the brand and the educator is supply. Once you see the model, its outcomes stop being surprising.

Your brand disappears. Students enrol with the platform, not with you. If you leave, the students, the reviews and the history stay behind. You leave with nothing you can carry.

Your content becomes their asset. The lectures, notes and question banks you created keep selling the next batch, with or without you.

Your income is never fully yours. Revenue splits are rarely transparent, and the platform takes the larger share because it controls discovery.

Your growth is capped by their algorithm. The platform promotes whatever grows platform revenue. Some days that is you. Many days it is not. You are competing with your own platform's interests.

A platform that owns the student relationship will always, in the end, optimise for itself. That is not malice. It is gravity.

06The false choice

Ownership or reach. Never both, they said.

For half a decade, every option in Indian EdTech asked educators to give something up. Own your academy, and accept that you are on your own. Join a platform, and accept that you are a listing on someone else's shelf.

We think that choice was never real. It came from how the tools were built and how they made money, not from anything educators or students needed.

Ownership and reach are not opposites. They only looked that way because nobody built one thing that gave an educator both.

Look at how good shopkeepers work. A shop owner has their own signboard, their own counter, their own regular customers who come straight to them. They also sit in a market where new customers walk past every day. Nobody asks them to choose between their shop and the market. The shop is theirs. The market brings footfall. And the more good shops a market has, the more people come to it, which helps every shop in it.

Educators deserve the same arrangement. Their own academy, on their own domain, under their own name, built and run by them. And alongside it, a shared marketplace where students who are looking for a teacher can find them, so that every educator who joins makes it a little easier for every other educator to be discovered.

Notice the order. The academy comes first. Discovery supports it. A marketplace that replaces the academy is just another platform. A marketplace that sends students to academies is a public good for teachers.

That is the layer Indian education has been missing. Not another app. Not another platform. A foundation that lets each educator build their own academy, and quietly connects all of them.

07The Operating System of Education

You build the academy. We run everything beneath it.

We describe AllCoaching as the Operating System of Education deliberately. Not an app-building agency. Not a platform that rents you a shelf. An operating system.

An operating system does not decide what you build on it. It does the invisible work underneath, so that you can build exactly what you want on top. Nobody thinks about the operating system on their phone while they are calling their mother. That is the point. It is there so that the important thing can happen without friction.

For an educator, the important thing is teaching. So AllCoaching is built as layers that run underneath every academy, the same way for a single tutor and for a multi-teacher institute.

Under borrowed ground, value leaks to whoever holds the keys. On owned ground, it compounds for the person doing the work, and through them, for the students they teach.

09The next decade

Every educator an institution.

A manifesto should be clear about the line between what exists and what it hopes for. So here it is, drawn plainly.

What follows under Today is true now. What follows under Next and The decade ahead is belief and direction: the world we think Indian education is moving towards, and the world we intend to help build. None of it is a product announcement.

TodayTrue now

An educator can own an academy in an afternoon.

The tools that once needed an agency and a quote are now self-serve. That alone is a break from every era before it.

NextBelief & direction

The small-town academy stops looking small.

We believe the gap between a single teacher and a large institution will close, not because the institutions shrink, but because the teacher's infrastructure grows up.

  • Every educator an institution. One teacher with the systems that only big institutes used to afford: admissions, payments, classes, records, an app, a website, all running together.
  • A teacher in a small town runs an academy as professional as the biggest brands. The student cannot tell from the screen whether the academy has one teacher or a hundred. They can only tell whether the teaching is good.
  • Technology disappears into the background. Setting up, getting paid and giving access become as unremarkable as electricity. The teacher only teaches.
  • Students choose teachers by quality, not ad budget. As more good academies are discoverable side by side, reputation earned in the classroom starts to outweigh reach bought in a feed.
The decade aheadBelief & direction

A network of academies, not a handful of giants.

Ten years from now, we believe Indian education will look less like a few towers and more like a city: millions of independent academies, each with its own door, connected by shared streets.

  • Education as a network of independent academies. Not a few giant platforms deciding who is seen, but a mesh of educator-owned academies where students move freely to whoever teaches them best.
  • Regional and Hindi-medium educators become national names. The finest teacher of a subject in Hindi, Bengali, Tamil or Marathi should be reachable by every student who learns in that language, wherever they live.
  • AI as the educator's assistant, never their replacement. We believe new tools should take the drudgery off a teacher's desk so they have more time to teach, and that the relationship between a student and a teacher should stay human. This is a belief about how such tools should be used, not a feature we are announcing.
  • An educator's brand and students belong to the educator, for life. A reputation built over decades should never be stranded inside someone else's product. It should move with the teacher, the way a degree or a good name does.

None of this happens because one company wills it. It happens because enough educators decide, one academy at a time, that they will no longer teach on borrowed ground. Our job is to make that decision easy, and to keep the infrastructure honest while they make it.

10The manifesto

Our promise to every educator who ever deserved more.

Seven beliefs we were founded on, and three we are adding as we look at the decade ahead. Hold us to all ten.

01

We believe teaching is a vocation, not a startup.

An educator should never have to become a developer, a marketer or a payments desk to run an academy. The technology should disappear so the teaching can come first.

02

We believe every educator should own their academy.

Their name on the website, their logo on the app, their students in their list, their data exportable at any time. An educator's brand is not a listing on someone else's shelf.

03

We believe an educator should be able to build it themselves.

No sales call, no demo, no agency, no waiting. If an educator can teach a class, they can build their academy in a few clicks, and change it whenever they want.

04

We believe ownership and reach should not be a trade-off.

An educator should have their own academy and still be found by students who don't know them yet. Every educator who joins AllCoaching makes the marketplace stronger for every other educator.

05

We believe a student should find the best teacher, not just the most advertised one.

The best biology teacher in Kanpur should be reachable by a student in Kochi. Distance and ad budgets should stop deciding who gets found. Merit should.

06

We believe prices should be published before anyone signs up.

What we charge and what the educator keeps is written down in the open. One published plan price, 0% commission on Growth and Pro, and no surprises after the fact.

07

We are honest about what we are building and what it will take.

AllCoaching is not a magic solution. Having your own academy doesn't guarantee overnight success. What it guarantees is a fair foundation, one where your success depends on the quality of your teaching, not on your budget or technical skills. We give you the tools. The rest is yours to win.

08

We believe technology should be invisible.

The best infrastructure is the kind nobody notices. If an educator is thinking about servers, app stores or payment gateways, we have not done our job yet.

09

We believe an educator's students belong to the educator.

The relationship between a teacher and the students they taught is not inventory. It is not ours to keep, sell or redirect. It goes wherever the educator goes.

10

We believe reach should be earned by teaching, not bought.

Advertising will always exist, and educators are free to use it. But a teacher who teaches well should be able to grow without outbidding anyone. Good teaching should be the strongest signal there is.
